Knowledge Ops & Governance Framework

A governance framework for a 9-person knowledge team whose entire publication pipeline depends on SME reviewers who owe the team no formal time. Nine controlled statuses, each with one named owner, observable entry and exit criteria, and required evidence — designed after a duplicated article and two confused new hires showed that ownership existed as suggestion, not control. Endorsed by the Team Lead; partially implemented, with full rollout targeted for Q1 2027.

02 — The problem

The problem

Two analysts independently drafted articles from the same source document — caught before publication, but at the cost of duplicated authoring and duplicated SME review time, the scarcest resource in the pipeline. Nobody could answer “why are there two of these,” because there was no intake control and no single record of ownership. Separately, two new hires exposed the same gap from another angle: The team couldn’t explain its own process or where any article stood, because there was no structure to explain it with. One failure cost money and expert time; the other costs the team’s ability to describe itself, and recurs with every hire.

03 — Constraints

Constraints

  • SME review gates every article, and the KM team has no authority over reviewer time — every review is a favor, since reviewers are primarily support operators.
  • Dedicated review support scaled to only one product area; it can’t be requested everywhere.
  • Existing ownership language used “or” between roles, which meant no one was actually accountable.
  • Service commitments (e.g., a 3-day review turnaround) existed as examples with no enforcement or escalation behind them.
  • The tracker couldn’t yet timestamp status transitions, which blocks measuring cycle time until it’s fixed.
  • Any new commitment on reviewers had to be realistic enough that a busy SME could actually honor it.
04 — The design

The design

Nine statuses, each a controlled checkpoint: Authoring · For SME Review · For Revision · Ready with Edits · Ready for Migration · Good for Publish · Published · Cancelled · Already in Product Docs. Every status carries entry criteria, one named owner, exit criteria, required evidence, and allowed next statuses — observable criteria only (“has passed self-review and been assigned to the correct reviewer,” not “is mostly ready”). A later redesign inserts an In SME Review status once the SME actually starts reviewing — separating queue wait from active review time, which the current single status conflates — and proposes a Migrated status to replace Good for Publish with a more precisely defined state. Both are under observation and not yet part of the core nine.

One owner per status, no “or.” Where an owner genuinely wasn’t decided, the document carries a visible placeholder rather than a guessed answer.

Evidence as a control, not a “should.” A status change without its required evidence is invalid and gets reverted — this is what turns the tracker into an audit trail instead of a self-reported board.

Acknowledgement split from decision. Where a product has a dedicated reviewer: One business day to acknowledge, three to decide, up to three follow-ups spaced three days apart. Everywhere else — most products, since reviewers are support operators juggling other work — only acknowledgement is asked; no hard decision deadline is imposed. An acknowledgement a busy expert can honor; a forced decision often can’t be, so only the enforceable half gets committed.

The follow-up rule. Follow up only after the date the reviewer themselves committed to, never before. If still open, close the intake ticket (not the article, which keeps its lifecycle status), move on, and circle back on a scheduled follow-up — instead of holding capacity open through an unbounded renegotiation loop.

Throughput as a rate, not a fixed number. Completed articles ÷ available writer-weeks (headcount minus absence and overhead). A rate reads a short-staffed week as arithmetic, not failure, and stays comparable as staffing changes.

Five tracker-derived metrics, reported monthly — no new logging burden, since an abandoned metric produces confidently wrong data.

05 — One status, in full

One status, in full

Document excerpt

For SME Review

The status the team controls least, so it had to be defined most carefully.

Entry
Draft complete, self-reviewed, correct SME assigned, technically mature enough for meaningful review.
Owner
SME (writer stays available for questions).
Exit
Recorded approval · approval with minor edits · actionable revision feedback · cancel/pivot decision · confirmation equivalent content already exists. Silence is not approval.
Evidence required
At least one of an approval comment, an approval email or message, source-document review comments, a documented revision or cancellation decision, or a link to the existing article.
Allowed next
Ready for Migration Ready with Edits For Revision Cancelled Already in Product Docs

The entry criteria protect reviewer time; the evidence list makes approval retrievable; the allowed transitions mean nothing skips straight to publication. The other eight statuses are specified to the same depth.

06 — Key decisions

Key decisions

Left post-publication statuses unwritten. Maintenance, retirement, and archiving stay undesigned until one real maintenance cycle has run by hand — a status for work nobody’s done yet gets used by nobody.

No quality metrics in phase one. A quality metric on an unstable process just measures the instability; visibility and measurement had to come first.

Anchored publication targets to the tax season cycle, not internal preference, since that peak is also the only window with enough traffic to measure whether articles actually close support gaps.

07 — What’s already mechanized

What’s already mechanized

Parts of this model run as code today. The Claude Cowork drafting pipeline (its own case study) enforces two of its exception rules directly: Two consecutive failures on one file stop the run, and a source spanning multiple topics stops for an approved split plan before drafting begins — the same triage control that would have prevented the original duplicate. Every draft also carries a mandatory review tail (flags, structure suggestions, changes) so uncertainty surfaces as a reviewable item instead of being resolved silently. Same principle either place: Controls that can be mechanized get mechanized in code; controls that need judgment get written down and assigned to a person instead.

08 — What changed after testing

What changed after testing

  • Split ownership language to remove every “or” after tracing how accountability died inside it.
  • Made evidence a hard gate instead of a “should,” after the tracker drifted from reality without one.
  • Split acknowledgement from decision commitments once it was clear a forced deadline on a favor-based reviewer would be ignored.
  • Added the follow-up rule after watching a promised date slip into an unbounded renegotiation loop.
  • Deferred quality metrics and post-publication statuses rather than design ahead of real usage data.
09 — Limitations and next step

Limitations and next step

Endorsed but only partially implemented — retrofitted onto the current tracker, with full rollout targeted for Q1 2027 while pieces are incorporated incrementally in the meantime. Measurement is blocked until the tracker logs status-change timestamps automatically (see the open item below); until then, cycle time and reviewer wait time can’t be measured at all. In hindsight, the measurement layer (commitment table, five indicators) was specified before the tracker could measure anything — instrumentation should have shipped first. A 70-term glossary was written as a maintained artifact when only about a dozen terms actually carry rules; the rest was a one-time alignment exercise, not an asset to maintain. Next: Ship the tracker timestamping, then validate wait time, rework rate, and duplicate/cancellation rate against the pre-framework baseline.
